## People management

*"You haven’t managed many people. How are you ready?"*

> I’d separate formal people management from leadership. I’ve formally supervised three buyers, but I’ve spent my career leading cross-functional initiatives — Finance, Legal, IT, suppliers, executives — where I had no direct authority. That teaches you something specific: you can’t get outcomes just by telling people what to do. You have to create clarity on the outcome, give context, remove blockers, and help people make better decisions themselves.
>
> My approach would be simple: clear expectations, high ownership, frequent real feedback, very little micromanagement. And I’d approach hiring and developing this specialist team very intentionally rather than assuming I already have the perfect management playbook.

*If pushed — underperformer:*

> First question isn’t “how do I fix this.” It’s “do they know what excellent looks like, do they have the capability, and have I given them the tools and feedback to succeed?” Then: skill gap → coach; clarity gap → clarify; capacity issue → fix prioritization; accountability issue → address directly. In that order.

## “Unique process” trap

*"What if a customer says their procurement process is unique?"*

> I’d first determine whether the process is actually unique or whether they’ve accumulated exceptions over time that no one ever challenged.
>
> Then I’d bucket requirements into three categories: things that are truly business-critical, things that are compliance and accounting requirements, and things that are simply how they’ve always done it.
>
> I’d design around the first two and challenge the third. That’s how you build something that works long-term instead of a one-off configuration nobody else can support.

## Playbook (job-winning)

*"How would you build the Procurement Activation playbook?"*

> I’d build it around the customer journey, not around Ramp’s internal org.
>
> Four stages: discovery, solution design, implementation, and go-live + optimization.
>
> Discovery: understand current P2P, stakeholders, approval structure, ERP, AP process, pain points, success metrics.
>
> Solution design: define intake, approval routing logic, vendor workflow, PO requirements, accounting dimensions, integrations, exception handling.
>
> Implementation: repeatable checklist — configuration, customer responsibilities, testing, training, launch criteria. No implementation should feel different unless complexity requires it.
>
> Go-live and optimization: time-to-first-request, adoption, approval cycle time, exception rate, invoice match rate, CSAT. And after each implementation: what surprised us, what took longest, what confused customers, what becomes standard.
>
> Once we have enough implementations, I’d build a maturity model: what an Activation Specialist handles independently, what requires escalation, what are the common failure modes. That’s how individual expertise becomes a scalable team.

## Product feedback

> I wouldn’t send Product a list of feature requests. I’d categorize feedback into bugs, missing functionality, UX friction, and feature requests. If three customers ask for something, that’s anecdotal. If thirty percent of implementations hit the same blocker, that’s a product signal. I’d bring pattern plus frequency plus business impact plus proposed solution.

---

## Sales handoff

> The activation team’s job shouldn’t start when the contract is signed. I want a standardized handoff that includes current procurement process, ERP, key stakeholders, scope, complexity, required integrations, risks, and agreed success criteria. A bad handoff creates downstream implementation problems that look like activation problems.
